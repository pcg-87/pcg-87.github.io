*First in a series. This one is just my starting position — what I thought was happening and why, written down before I checked any of it.*

## The thesis I walked in with

Private equity has become a bigger and bigger part of the economy. Buying houses. Rolling up services like trash, HVAC, plumbing — all the small essential services of everyday life. Health care. Elder care. Slowly financializing it.

The story I had was simple: they roll up a market, that gives them pricing power, and they use it to drive our everyday costs up and make the product shittier.

It didn't feel like a fringe worry. By the industry's own accounting, PE-backed companies directly employ 13.3 million people in the US and account for roughly 7% of GDP. Those are the lobby's numbers, so treat them as an upper bound — but even discounted, this isn't a niche.

## The trade-offs

There are a couple of reasons I couldn't just be against it.

**Retirement.** A lot of pension funds are invested in private equity to get extra return. Public pension plans hold somewhere between 10% and 14% of their assets in it, depending on whose count you use. Alternatives as a whole went from 9% of public pension portfolios in 2001 to 34% by 2024. So if you destroy those stable returns, how do you balance retirees' need for growth and stability when they're not earning income anymore?

**The legitimate version.** PE in its best sense is supposed to be a forcing function. It helps owners exit who don't have a natural heir. It turns around underperforming businesses. Those are real problems that somebody has to solve, and there isn't an obvious queue of other buyers.

## What I suspected anyway

Despite the need for returns, my suspicion was that the extractive nature of PE may still come out as a net negative for society overall.

And I didn't think lower returns would settle it either way. If the returns come in below what a pension assumed, the shortfall doesn't disappear — it lands on the guarantors. More taxes.

So this might be a false dichotomy. I couldn't tell whether that was true or whether I just wanted it to be.

## What I hadn't examined

1. Buying houses and rolling up HVAC are the same phenomenon.
2. Pricing power comes from consolidating market share.
3. The pension leg and the consumer leg are a real trade — one buys the other.

## How I decided to test it

Rather than argue about private equity in general, I took markets one at a time and asked the same questions of each:

- Who actually pays, and is the price set by the market or by someone else?
- What alternatives does the buyer have at the moment they buy?
- Where does the money come out — price, quality, or somewhere else entirely?

Nursing homes first, because the evidence there is the best. Then the trades — HVAC, fire alarm service, auto repair. Then homes and rent. Then doctors' practices. Then back to the money itself: how the returns get made, who bears the cost, and whether any rule could reach it.

I'll say this much now: I didn't end up where I started.
